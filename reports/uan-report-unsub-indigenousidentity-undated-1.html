Colonization, Colonialism and Coloniality

↑ Contents

European coloniality⁹—a mindset of civilizational superiority rooted in Christianity and racial hierarchies—fundamentally disrupted Bharat’s indigenous consciousness, legal systems, and political structures.

Despite political independence in 1947, coloniality persists in India’s institutions, particularly the Constitution, which retains Eurocentric frameworks.

Colonization refers to the process by which a dominant power establishes colonies in foreign societies while maintaining political and economic ties to its homeland.

Colonialism, in turn, represents the broader policy of using colonization as a means to expand influence, extract resources, and exert control over subjugated territories.

However, beyond the tangible structures of colonial rule lies coloniality—the deeper ideological framework that legitimizes and perpetuates colonialism through cultural domination, epistemic control, and the systematic reshaping of colonized societies in the image of the colonizer.

This mindset outlasts formal colonial rule, embedding itself in institutions, education, and collective consciousness.

Decoloniality emerges as the necessary response to coloniality, challenging its hegemony by reclaiming indigenous worldviews, restoring agency to colonized peoples, and dismantling the enduring legacies of imperial thought.

European coloniality introduced two particularly damaging frameworks that continue to shape global power structures.

First, the Modernity/Rationality Complex systematically portrayed European thought as universally valid and progressive while labeling indigenous knowledge systems as primitive or backward.

Second, colonial powers weaponized race and religion as tools of domination - Christianity was used to justify colonization as a "civilizing mission," while manufactured racial hierarchies like the Aryan-Dravidian theory were employed to fracture Bharat's civilizational unity and justify foreign rule.

European history was positioned as the universal narrative, while non-European cultures were relegated to the margins of "pre-modernity."

Language itself became a tool of domination: terms like "modern" and "rational" were weaponized to delegitimize alternative worldviews, cementing a civilizational hierarchy where European ways were unquestioningly deemed superior.

The colonial encounter fundamentally disrupted Bharat's civilizational fabric across multiple dimensions.

In the religious sphere, Christian missionaries and British administrators deliberately redefined Hinduism through a Christian lens, imposing foreign categories like "religion" that distorted its inherently Dharmic, cosmic-order-based nature.

This process included projecting Protestant anti-clericalism onto Brahmins, portraying them as oppressive priests in need of reform.

The education system became a key battleground, where colonial policies systematically erased indigenous epistemologies - including gurukuls and oral traditions - replacing them with Eurocentric curricula that positioned English as the sole language of power and intellectual legitimacy.

Following the 1648 Peace of Westphalia, European powers constructed an international legal order that defined "civilized nations" exclusively as those adhering to Christian-secular norms.

This deliberate Eurocentric standard served to justify colonial domination over societies like Bharat, which were deemed "uncivilized" precisely because they operated on different civilizational premises.

Far from being neutral or objective, this framework directly influenced India's constitutional development, ensuring that even after independence, governance structures remained continuations of colonial models rather than authentic expressions of indigenous political thought.
