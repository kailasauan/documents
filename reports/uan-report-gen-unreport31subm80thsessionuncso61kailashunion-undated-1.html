3.3. Legal Persecution

Enacted during colonial rule on November 25, 1870, Section 124A of the Indian Penal Code (IPC) was designed to suppress dissent against the British government.

Today, it is often criticized for being a tool of political repression.

Of the nearly 11,000 people charged in 816 sedition cases since 2010, 65% were charged after the Modi government came to power in 2014.[28]

One significant case is that of Saint Rampal, a global Hindu spiritual leader who founded the Kabir Panth-affiliated Satlok Ashram in Haryana.[29]

The Haryana government charged Saint Rampal and his followers with sedition under Section 124A of the IPC in November 2014.

The Supreme Court’s landmark ruling in Kedar Nath Singh v. State of Bihar (1962) clarified that sedition applies only to acts with a clear intent or tendency to incite violence or public disorder against the government, safeguarding free speech unless it crosses this threshold.

In Saint Rampal’s case, the standoff was a reaction to a specific legal summons rather than a coordinated effort to overthrow the government. No evidence has surfaced of speeches or writings explicitly calling for rebellion or hatred against the government as an institution.[30]

The Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), which came into effect on July 1, 2024, replaced the colonial-era Indian Penal Code (IPC).

Unlike the IPC, which focused on disaffection toward the government, Section 152 broadens the offense to include acts threatening the abstract concepts of "sovereignty, unity, and integrity of India."[31]

National Crime Records Bureau (NCRB) data from 2015–2020 shows 548 arrests under Section 124A, with only 12 convictions in seven cases[32], highlighting a low conviction rate and suggesting frequent misuse as a tool for harassment rather than prosecution.
