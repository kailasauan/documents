This submission by Kailash Union responds to the request of the Human Rights Council in Resolution 57/28, which called upon the UN High Commissioner for Human Rights to conduct a global analytical study on the implementation of a human rights-based approach (HRBA) in the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (GBF).

Consistent with Section C of the GBF and the outcomes of CBD COP16, this report provides examples, data, and analysis on the United States of KAILASA's integration of human rights in biodiversity strategies, measures to protect traditional knowledge, monitoring mechanisms, identified challenges, and recommendations.

KAILASA recognizes that biodiversity loss disproportionately impacts Indigenous Peoples, local communities, and vulnerable groups.

Our approach to GBF implementation prioritizes the respect, protection, and fulfillment of human rights – particularly the rights to participation, Free, Prior and Informed Consent (FPIC), and equitable benefit-sharing.

Alignment with Human Rights and the GBF

The GBF explicitly recognizes indigenous and traditional knowledge as central.

Section C of the GBF affirms that indigenous peoples and local communities are “custodians of biodiversity” whose rights, knowledge and values must be “respected, documented and preserved with their free, prior and informed consent”.

It acknowledges that diverse value systems (like seeing Earth as sacred) are integral to success.

Similarly, the Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD) encourages Parties to honor Article 8(j), preserving Indigenous knowledge in conservation.

The practices of KAILASA, an indigenous nation, are a concrete application of this mandate, and embed traditional knowledge documentation into site management.

Research affirms this approach: land managed by Indigenous peoples retains more biodiversity than other areas.

For example, UNDP notes that Indigenous lands contain much of the world’s biodiversity and carbon, and are often in better ecological health.

A human-rights-based approach to biodiversity (HRBA) yields better outcomes for the people and planet.

KAILASA’s model follows this: by giving communities ownership of sacred sites and farming practices, it reinforces rights to land, food and culture while conserving ecosystems.

Such community-led restoration increases plant diversity, stabilizes soils and watersheds, and improves livelihoods.
