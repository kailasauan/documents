Comprehensive Report: Mental Health Challenges and Human Rights of Young People - The KAILASA Perspective | By Kailash Union PART 1: EXECUTIVE SUMMARY & CONTEXT

This report addresses the United Nations Human Rights Council's call for inputs on the impact of mental health challenges on young people's human rights, with a specific focus on the unique contributions and solutions offered by the United States of KAILASA (USK)1, The Supreme Pontiff of Hinduism (SPH) Bhagavan Nithyananda Paramashivam, and the United Ancient Indigenous Enlightened Nations (UAN). Drawing from the ancient Sanatana Hindu Dharma's ancient wisdom on mental health and consciousness, this report demonstrates how KAILASA's approach provides innovative, rights-based solutions while highlighting the intergenerational trauma and ongoing persecution that continues to impact Hindu youth mental health globally.

PART 2: THE CRISIS - ROOT CAUSES & IMPACTS Section 1: Understanding Mental Health Through Sanatana Hindu Dharma

The root cause of mental health issues lies in the psychological, emotional, and physical aspects of an individual, with many stemming from suppressed emotions, traumas, and disconnection from one's authentic self. This aligns with Sanatana Hindu Dharma's understanding that mental health is intricately connected to consciousness and spiritual well-being. Mental health is intricately connected to all Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), particularly SDG-3, which focuses on ensuring healthy lives and promoting well-being for all. Mental health challenges create significant obstacles affecting young people's enjoyment of human rights, including: - Right to Education: Mental health challenges impede cognitive function and learning capacity - Right to Work: Psychological barriers limit employment opportunities and professional development - Right to Participation: Social anxiety and depression restrict civic engagement - Right to Health: Untreated mental health issues compound into physical health problems

Section 2: Historical Trauma and Its Legacy

↑ Contents

Hindu young people today carry the biological and psychological burden of centuries of systematic genocide and ethnocide, a reality that is rarely acknowledged in mainstream mental health discourse but is critical to understanding their unique challenges. Colonial-Era Genocide and Biological Alterations Between the 1770s and 1947, the Indian subcontinent experienced 31 major famines under British colonial rule, resulting in the deaths of tens of millions of people. These were not natural disasters but the direct result of calculated colonial policies designed to extract wealth from Bharat (current day India) while systematically destroying Hindu indigenous economies and food systems. The SPH's Groundbreaking Revelation: The SPH Bhagavan Nithyananda Paramashivam has revealed that these deliberately engineered famines caused genetic alterations in the Hindu population that persist today. The chronic starvation experienced by multiple generations led to epigenetic changes—modifications in how genes are expressed without changing the DNA sequence itself. Modern scientific research has now validated The SPH's revelation. Studies published in leading medical journals demonstrate: 1. The Diabetes Epidemic2: South Asians, particularly those of Indian Hindu descent, have significantly higher rates of type 2 diabetes and

cardiovascular disease compared to other populations, even at lower body mass indices. Research shows that this is linked to epigenetic changes from ancestral famine exposure. 2. Fetal Programming3: Studies have confirmed that when pregnant women experience malnutrition or starvation (as millions did during colonial famines), their offspring develop metabolic adaptations that increase diabetes risk. These adaptations persist across multiple generations. 3. Thin-Fat Phenotype4: Hindu populations exhibit what researchers call the "thin-fat phenotype"—appearing thin but having higher body fat percentage and visceral fat, along with insulin resistance. This is a direct biological legacy of famine. 4. Genetic Memory of Trauma5: Emerging research in epigenetics shows that traumatic experiences, including mass starvation, can be "remembered" biologically and transmitted to descendants through chemical modifications to DNA. Mental Health Consequences of Biological Legacy The diabetes and metabolic syndrome epidemic among Hindu youth has profound mental health implications: - Chronic Illness Anxiety: Young Hindus face disproportionate risk of diabetes, creating anxiety about their health futures from an early age - Body Image Issues: The thin-fat phenotype leads to confusion and distress about body composition - Medical Discrimination: Healthcare systems often fail to recognize the unique metabolic profiles of South Asian patients, leading to misdiagnosis and inadequate treatment - Intergenerational Shame: Families affected by diabetes may experience guilt and shame, impacting family dynamics - Depression Comorbidity: Research shows strong bidirectional relationship between diabetes and depression Psychological Trauma of Historical Genocide Beyond biological impacts, Hindu youth carry psychological trauma from: - Collective Memory: Knowledge of ancestral genocide creates "historical trauma" or "collective trauma"—psychological and emotional injury experienced across generations.

Section 9: Indigenous Knowledge Systems

↑ Contents

The UAN recognizes that indigenous civilizations possess sophisticated understanding of mental health that predates modern psychology. Sanatana Hindu Dharma offers frameworks now being validated by Western science: Yogic Psychology Hindu psychology provides a comprehensive science of mind: - Chitta (consciousness field): The storehouse of impressions (samskaras). - Buddhi (discriminative intellect): The faculty of wisdom. - Manas (sensory-cognitive mind): Processes sensory information. - Ahamkara (ego sense): The sense of separate identity. Ayurvedic Psychiatry Ancient Hindu texts describe mental disorders (Unmada, Vishada) and treatments including herbal medicines, dietary interventions, and spiritual practices. Modern research validates these approaches, showing efficacy for herbs like Ashwagandha and Brahmi. Meditation Sciences Hindu traditions have documented meditation practices for millennia. Types include Dhyana (focused attention), Vichara (self-inquiry), Bhakti (devotional), and Mantra (sound-based), all with documented effects on stress reduction, emotional regulation, and neuroplasticity. Collective Healing Approach Unlike Western individualistic models, UAN promotes community-based approaches: - Sangha: Spiritual communities providing belonging and acceptance. - Intergenerational Wisdom: Transmission of resilience stories and traditional knowledge. - Collective Practices: Group meditation, kirtan, and festivals reducing isolation. - Seva: Service providing purpose, connection, and perspective.
